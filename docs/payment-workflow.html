AffiliatePoppy · design note · 16 August 2026

How the money moves

Every payment path, every date, and exactly what the ledger shows — including the ones where something goes wrong. Built from decisions D12 and D15–D15e.

Read this first: there are two separate products here

Almost all the complexity lives in the second one, which is optional and not built. Keeping them apart is the difference between a simple story and a confusing one.

The sentence for an investor: the product we sell today counts money and never touches it. The platform-level programme — where AgentsPoppy stands behind every commission so a publisher only ever deals with one counterparty — is a designed, costed option we can switch on when it's worth the machinery.
World B · the unit

One sale, and where every euro goes

A €100 poppy, a 5% customer discount and a 10% publisher commission — the numbers AgentsPoppy uses on its own programme. Payments are Stripe direct charges: the customer's money lands in the developer's own Stripe account, and the platform's 5% is taken in that same instant. AgentsPoppy never holds the developer's revenue — which is what forces the whole design that follows.

Why AgentsPoppy pays, not the developer

So the publisher has one counterparty who can never answer "talk to the developer". That single promise is the reason a publisher trusts a programme they didn't sign up to individually — and it is the product.

Why the developer reimburses

Without it, AgentsPoppy nets −€4.75 on every campaign sale — paying €9.50 to earn €4.75. With it, the platform stays at its 5% and the developer pays for a sale they would not otherwise have had.

World B · timing

The calendar — a date, not a delay

Publishers do not need to be paid quickly. They need to know exactly when. So the schedule is a fixed calendar date, not a rolling countdown from each sale: sales in a month are paid on the 10th of the month after next.

What the wait is actually for

This is the part that is easy to get backwards. A longer wait does nothing about a developer who won't pay. That risk is governed by the order of the two events — collect first, pay second — and by the ability to stop issuing codes. What the wait buys is refund coverage: the window in which a sale can come undone before anyone has been paid. So the delay is set from the refund curve, and not one day longer.

RiskWhat actually controls itWhat does NOT help
Developer won't pay Collect before paying out; stop minting codes on failure Waiting longer
Sale gets refunded The clearing period — the entry cancels before money moves Any amount of paperwork
Debit reversed after payout Card or SEPA B2B mandate — never SEPA Core, which the payer can claw back for 8 weeks with no reason given Waiting longer (the window follows the debit, not the sale)
Card dispute at 120 days A small rolling reserve per publisher Making every publisher wait four months
The one thing deliberately left unprotected. If a developer's debit fails on the 1st, the publisher is still paid on the 10th. Making that payout conditional would put the publisher back to waiting on somebody else's money — the exact thing this design removes. Minting stops instead, so the loss is capped at one month of that one developer's affiliate sales.
Both worlds · the ledger

The life of a ledger entry

Platform exposure: only if the publisher never earns again. The deduction sits against future earnings, and a rolling reserve (one cycle held back) absorbs the case where they have already left. Both sides move symmetrically, so the platform is never left holding one leg of a reversed transaction.

4 A subscription renews, month after month

15 Mar
First payment. Commission earned.
15 Apr
Renewal. Carries no discount code — attribution rides the stored subscription link.
10 May
March's commission paid.
10 Jun
April's commission paid.
Recurring income, on the same calendar. Stripe sends no discount information on a renewal, so attribution is held by a subscription-to-publisher mapping written at the first sale. Each renewal is simply a new row on the normal schedule. The merchant can switch renewals off, in which case only the first payment ever earns.

5 The developer's direct debit fails

1 May
Debit declined. Retried, developer notified.
same day
Code minting stops for that developer. Existing codes keep working.
10 May
Publisher paid anyway — the promise holds.
1 Jun
Arrears collected with the next run, or the account is closed.
This is the one real loss case, and it is bounded. Worst case: one month of one developer's campaign sales. The publisher is never made to wait — that is deliberate, because a publisher who can be left unpaid by a stranger's default has no reason to trust the programme at all.

6 Someone tries the obvious fraud

A developer signs up as a publisher, "sells" their own poppy to themselves with their own code, collects the commission, then refunds the sale. Here is the arithmetic, wearing all three hats:

StepTheir position
Pays, as the fake customer−€95.00
Receives, as the merchant+€90.25
Receives, as the publisher+€9.50
Reimburses, as the developer−€9.50
Refund: gets the €95 back, returns the €90.25±€0.00
Reversal — a credit against future collections, not a payment out±€0.00
Stripe's processing fee, never returned−€1.70
Net result of the fraud−€6.45 per attempt
It loses money every time — by arithmetic, not by policy. The scheme only pays if a reversal can pull cash out of the platform, so reversals never do: the publisher takes a deduction, the developer takes a credit. It still loses money when two people collude and split the roles, because one of them has to be the developer paying in. On top of that: a developer may not be a publisher on their own poppy (both identities are known at minting time), and a refund pattern stops minting immediately.

7 The publisher has earned very little

10 May
Owed €7.20 — under the €25 threshold. Carried forward, visibly.
10 Jun
Now €18.60. Still carried.
10 Jul
€31.40 — paid in full.
A threshold, stated up front and always visible. The ledger shows the balance and how far it is from the next payout, so a small publisher is never left wondering whether they were forgotten. Without it, the platform pays bank fees to send €1.40 twelve times a year.

The five rules everything above follows from

  1. The party who issued the code pays the publisher. One counterparty, who can never say "talk to the developer".
  2. The developer reimburses that commission — automatically, by direct debit, on top of the platform's 5%. Nobody chases anybody.
  3. Collect before paying out. Nine days, not a month, and never the other way round.
  4. A fixed calendar date beats a short one. Sales in a month pay on the 10th of the month after next, and every row shows its date from birth.
  5. A reversal is a credit, never a payment out. This is what makes fraud arithmetically unprofitable rather than merely forbidden.
And the boundary, once more, because it is the thing to be clear about. Everything in this document describes central campaigns — the optional platform-level programme. AffiliatePoppy as it ships today does none of it: it counts commissions and shows the merchant what they owe, and the merchant pays their own publishers from their own bank. AgentsPoppy is not a party to any payment, holds no funds, and cannot be defrauded. The product can be sold, and this second half switched on later — or never.
